Part I — Research findings

FINDING 01

Does screen time relate to sleep quality?

Research question: Does screen time relate to sleep quality?

Evidence: Daily screen time vs. sleep quality: Pearson r = −0.747 (n = 15,000).

Analysis: A strong negative linear association appears in the data.

Interpretation: Higher screen time is associated with lower reported sleep quality; this does not establish causality.

FINDING 02

Is screen time associated with stress?

Research question: Is screen time associated with stress?

Evidence: Daily screen time vs. stress: Pearson r = +0.879 (n = 15,000).

Analysis: The positive association is strong.

Interpretation: Higher screen time corresponds to higher stress scores in this dataset, without proving screen time causes stress.

FINDING 03

Does sleep duration relate to stress?

Research question: Does sleep duration relate to stress?

Evidence: Sleep duration vs. stress: Pearson r = −0.005; p ≈ 0.55.

Analysis: There is no meaningful linear association in this sample.

Interpretation: Hours slept alone do not explain stress here; sleep quality may be a more informative measure.

FINDING 04

Do lower- and higher-screen-time groups differ?

Research question: Do lower- and higher-screen-time groups differ?

Evidence: Median screen time = 5.49 hours. Mean sleep quality: 7.35 vs. 5.14; mean stress: 4.83 vs. 9.13.

Analysis: Splitting at the sample median reveals substantial differences in both outcomes.

Interpretation: These are descriptive group comparisons, not causal effects; occupation and other factors may confound the differences.

FINDING 05

Is sleep quality more related to stress than sleep duration?

Research question: Is sleep quality more related to stress than sleep duration?

Evidence: Sleep quality vs. stress: r = −0.857; sleep duration vs. stress: r = −0.005.

Analysis: Sleep quality has a strong negative correlation with stress, whereas duration has essentially none.

Interpretation: Perceived sleep quality appears more informative about stress than sleep hours in this dataset.

Part II — Machine learning experiments

Experimental design

Task: predict continuous stress level from age, gender, occupation, daily screen time, pre-sleep phone use, sleep duration, caffeine intake, physical activity, and daily notifications. Sleep quality and mental fatigue are excluded from predictors to focus on behavioral and demographic variables and avoid using closely related outcome measures.

Method: random 80% training / 20% testing split (seed 42); training-only preprocessing using median imputation, standardization of numeric variables, and one-hot encoding of categorical variables. All ten models use the same split. No hyperparameter search was performed; results are a baseline comparison.

Metrics: R² (higher is better), MAE and RMSE (lower is better). Metrics are reported on the held-out test set.

Best performer: Gradient Boosting (R² 0.8190, RMSE 1.1673). Its flexible nonlinear relationships may explain its advantage over linear baselines. The improvement is observational and specific to this split, not proof of generalization to other populations.

Limitations & interpretation

Correlations and model accuracy do not prove causation. This dataset includes several occupations, not only students. The dataset has no time index, so temporal trends cannot be tested; cross-sectional relationships are evaluated instead. No external validation set is available. Extremely small p-values from large samples do not alone imply practical importance.
