01 / Research introduction · Assignment requirements

Research Topic, Problem & Hypotheses

An introduction to the study and the eight required components of the research proposal.

1. Research topic

The Relationship Between Screen Time, Sleep, and Stress in People. This study explores patterns connecting daily device use, sleep duration, sleep quality, and perceived stress across 15,000 dataset records.

2. Kaggle dataset search and selection

Research source: Sleep, Screen Time and Stress Analysis on Kaggle. The website analyzes the accompanying uploaded CSV dataset (sleep_mobile_stress_dataset_15000), which contains 15,000 records and fields for screen use, sleep, stress, and related lifestyle characteristics. The linked Kaggle notebook is a research reference; the exact provenance of the uploaded CSV should be confirmed separately.

3. Real-world problem

Phones, computers, and other screens are part of everyday work, school, and leisure. Heavy screen use may coincide with poor sleep quality and elevated stress, but the size and direction of these relationships are not always obvious. This study examines whether people reporting different levels of screen time also report different sleep and stress outcomes.

4. Why this problem matters

Sleep and stress influence daily functioning, concentration, mood, and overall well-being. Understanding patterns in screen use can help people ask better questions about digital habits and guide future research into healthier routines. Because this dataset is observational, the findings identify associations rather than proving that screens cause changes in sleep or stress.

5. Primary research question

How is daily screen time associated with sleep duration, sleep quality, and perceived stress among people represented in this dataset?

6. Research sub-questions

  1. How does sleep quality differ across daily screen-time levels?
  2. Is greater screen time associated with higher perceived stress?
  3. Is daily screen time related to nightly sleep duration?
  4. Does phone use before bedtime relate to sleep quality?
  5. How do screen-time, sleep, and stress patterns vary by occupation or other recorded characteristics?

7. Potential variables

Main predictor: daily screen time (hours). Main outcomes: sleep duration (hours), sleep quality score, and stress level. Additional explanatory or grouping variables: phone use before sleep (minutes), age, gender, occupation, caffeine intake (cups), physical activity (minutes), daily notifications, and mental fatigue score. These are fields available in the uploaded CSV.

8. Preliminary hypotheses

H1: People with more daily screen time will tend to report higher stress scores.

H2: People with more daily screen time will tend to report lower sleep-quality scores.

H3: More daily screen time will be associated with shorter nightly sleep duration. These are testable predictions, not assumed results; the analysis may support or fail to support each hypothesis.

03 / Required exploratory analysis

Distributions, Differences, Relationships & Data Quality

Eight required investigations using the supplied 15,000-row dataset. The conclusions below are descriptive associations, not causal claims.

01 / 08

Distributions

Daily screen time averages 5.50 hours, sleep duration averages 6.51 hours, and stress averages 6.98. Figures 1–3 show the overall spread of each variable.

02 / 08

Group differences

People with more than 6 screen hours average 9.34 stress points, compared with 3.42 for those at 3 hours or less. Figures 7–9 and 12–13 compare groups.

03 / 08

Relationships

Higher screen exposure is associated with worse sleep quality and higher stress. Figures 4–6 compare individual observations; the relationships do not establish which factor causes another.

04 / 08

Correlations

Pearson r for screen time and stress is +0.879; for screen time and sleep quality it is -0.747; for screen time and sleep duration it is -0.002. See Figure 10.

05 / 08

Trends

Average stress rises and average sleep quality falls across increasing screen-time bands (Figures 7, 8 and 12). The data has no date or timestamp column, so a genuine time-series trend cannot be established.

06 / 08

Outliers

Using the 1.5×IQR rule, flagged values are: screen time 0, sleep duration 0, sleep quality 9, stress 0, and mental fatigue 0. Figure 11 illustrates stress spread and potential extremes; outliers are not automatically errors.

07 / 08

Missing-data patterns

The source CSV has 0 missing cells across 15,000 rows and 13 columns. There are no observed missing-data patterns in this file.

08 / 08

Potential anomalies

The source has 0 exact duplicate rows. Stress reaches its recorded maximum of 10; 3,875 records sit at that ceiling, which may compress differences among highly stressed participants. This is a measurement limitation, not evidence of invalid data.

Method note: Quartiles and IQR outlier counts use the raw supplied CSV. The dataset is cross-sectional, so these analyses describe associations rather than changes over calendar time.

03 / Insights

What the dataset suggests

Screen time & stress

Pearson r = +0.879: a strong positive linear association in these records.

Screen time & sleep quality

Pearson r = -0.747: a strong negative linear association in these records.

Screen time & sleep duration

Pearson r = -0.002: little evidence of a linear association.

Important limitation

These are observational, cross-sectional records. Correlation does not prove that screen time causes stress or poorer sleep.

04 / Methodology

Dataset and preparation

Source: uploaded Sleep, Mobile & Stress dataset (15,000 rows, 13 columns). Distributions, group averages and Pearson correlations were calculated from the uploaded CSV. The source has no dates, so a time-series chart would be misleading.

Cleaning documentation, the prepared CSV, SQLite database, and eight SQL query outputs from the earlier assignment remain included in this website package. The data's sampling method and measurement validity were not independently verified.
